# The clinician check — how to run it

**Read this first; hand over the other page second.** `docs/ward-flow-clinician-check.md` is written
for the clinician and is complete. This page is written for the owner and is about method: who to
ask, in what order, and what to decide before going in. Two audiences, two pages — not two homes for
one fact.

**Why it needs a method at all.** The check has been deferred twice and the model has been revised
twice without it. Every revision makes a "you have this wrong" answer more expensive, because more
now rests on it: a calculation layer, a seeded ward, a board screen and ninety-odd decisions. The
risk is no longer that the check does not happen. It is that it happens and comes back "yes" for
reasons that have nothing to do with whether the model is right.

---

## Who

**A nurse unit manager.** They own the bed, they live the daily reality, and they are the person who
would have to keep this updated. A consultant sees the discharge decision but not the bed coming
free. A bed coordinator sees the network but not the ward floor. The model being checked is the
ward-side one, so the ward-side role is the right reader.

**Ask two, separately.** Two clinicians in one room converge on a single account within about ninety
seconds, and the convergence is invisible once it has happened. Asked separately, disagreement stays
visible — and two nurse unit managers disagreeing about how their own wards work would be the most
useful finding this check could produce.

---

## The ordering rule, which decides whether this is a check at all

**Ask the open question before showing anything.**

The clinician page opens by naming three stages. A reader who sees those first will fit their
experience into them — not from deference, but because that is how comprehension works. The result
is agreement that carries no information.

So open from memory, with nothing on the table:

> *"Walk me through what happens to a bed on your ward, from the moment someone decides a patient is
> going home, to the bed being free for the next person."*

Then stop talking.

**What you are listening for is whether a moment of DECIDING appears on its own**, distinct from
expecting. If it does, the three-stage model is confirmed by the strongest evidence available —
unprompted independent description. If instead the account is one continuous drift toward certainty,
that is the finding, and it is one you could never have heard after showing the page.

Hand over the document afterwards.

---

## The status gradient runs the wrong way, so ask for the exception

**A consultant psychiatrist is asking a nurse unit manager about ward work.** However collegial the
room, that gradient suppresses disagreement in exactly the direction that matters here — the
clinician has more to lose by contradicting the model than by going along with it, and going along
with it costs them nothing today.

This is the same failure as the ordering rule, arriving by a different route: both produce agreement
that carries no information.

**The counter is one sentence — ask for the exception, not the rule:**

> *"When does that not happen? Tell me about the last time it went differently."*

**Rules get agreement; exceptions get information.** A person describing a rule is describing what
they believe should happen, which is where the deference lives. A person describing last Tuesday is
describing what did happen, and the specifics arrive whether or not they support the model. Ask it
after every answer that sounded like a yes.

*(Contributed by the settings/process session, which had it and this page did not.)*

## What to listen for — five things, and the fifth is the one that gets missed

Not "do they agree". These:

1. **Do they use stages at all?** The model assumes discrete states. It may be continuous, or a
   single event with waiting either side.
2. **Is "confirmed" a moment or a drift?** The model treats it as a deliberate act with a time and a
   role. If it gradually becomes true, that stage is a fiction and several screens rest on it.
3. **Who actually decides?** The model says only the ward may move a bed between stages.
4. **Is "blocked" a stage or a flag?** Changed once already on the owner's own judgement, and never
   checked against a ward.
5. **What did they mention that the model has no place for?** — **the most valuable of the five and
   the easiest to miss, because it arrives as a digression.** A thing the model cannot represent will
   not present itself as an objection; it will present itself as an aside on the way to answering
   something else. Write down anything you found yourself mentally filing as "not relevant".

*(Contributed by the settings/process session.)*

## Decide what counts as "no" BEFORE you go in

Without this, every answer becomes "yes, with nuance", because that is what a generous colleague
gives you and what a hopeful author hears. Deciding the cost in advance is what stops the expensive
answer being talked away in the moment.

| If they say | Cost | What it means |
| ----------- | ---- | ------------- |
| **"Confirming isn't a separate act"** — it just gets gradually more certain | **Expensive** | The three-stage model collapses into a confidence scale, which this project deliberately moved away from. `WB-DB-2`, the confirmation fields, and the derivations resting on them all come back into question |
| **"Blocked is a stage, not a flag"** — once it is stuck it is no longer confirmed | **Moderate** | The counting changes. Note this is the exact defect already fixed once: a blocked release matched neither branch and was counted nowhere, so a ward's figures *improved* at the moment it got stuck |
| **"One date isn't enough"** — clinically ready and actually leaving are different days | **Cheap** | One extra field. Do not let its cheapness make it sound like agreement; it is still a "no" |
| **"Nobody would keep this updated daily"** | **Expensive, differently** | Not a modelling error. It means the design rests on work nobody has time for, which no amount of correct modelling fixes |

## What would waste it

**Explaining why it was built this way.** The moment the design is justified, the clinician evaluates
the reasoning rather than consulting their experience — and the reasoning is good, so they will agree
with it. That is the failure mode this check exists to avoid, arriving through the front door.

Say *"we might have this wrong, tell me if we have"*, and stop. The clinician page already carries a
note that an earlier version described four stages and was changed without a ward clinician seeing
it; that does the work better than anything said out loud, because it is evidence of being wrong
before rather than a promise to accept being wrong now.

---

## Practicalities

**Fifteen minutes, not five.** The page reads in five; the useful part of the conversation is at the
end, after the obvious answers are out of the way. Budgeting five guarantees you stop before it.

**Not by email. The pauses are data.** A hesitation before "yes, that's about right" is the finding,
and it does not survive being typed. This is also why it is not a review — it is one open question, a
page, and explicit permission to say the model is wrong.

*(Both contributed by the settings/process session, correcting this page's original "ten minutes".)*

---

## Afterwards

Record what came back in the ledger against `R3`, including a "yes" — a confirmed model is a result,
not the absence of one, and it is what closes the largest standing risk in the programme. If any
answer lands in the **expensive** row, stop and raise it before more is built on top; that is the
whole reason for doing this before the next phase rather than after it.
